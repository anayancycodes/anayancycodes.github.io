# Personal brand: ideas & a starter plan

A personal brand is just **what people reliably associate with your name**. You don't invent it — you choose a lane and show up in it consistently. Here's a plan shaped to where you are: early-career bioinformatics, learning in public, building an audience.

---

## 1. The positioning (your one sentence)

The strongest early-career brands aren't "expert in X." They're **"someone documenting the journey through X."** People follow journeys; they bookmark experts. Right now, the journey is your advantage — and it has a shelf life, so use it.

A working one-liner to grow into:

> **"Anayancy — learning bioinformatics in R, in public. I share the walkthroughs and the wrong turns."**

Why this works:
- **Specific** (bioinformatics + R, not "data" or "science")
- **Honest** (the wrong turns = trust, and they're easier to write than polished tutorials)
- **Useful** (someone six months behind you is your exact reader)

Pick one or two niches *inside* bioinformatics over time — e.g. single-cell RNA-seq, or R for genomics, or reproducible analysis. Narrow grows faster than broad.

---

## 2. Name & handle consistency

You're `anayancycodes` on GitHub and your site. **Grab that same handle everywhere you'll plausibly post**, even if you don't use them yet — it stops someone else taking it and makes you easy to find. Priority order:

1. **GitHub** ✅ (have it)
2. **LinkedIn** — highest ROI for early career + jobs. Use your real name; mention the blog.
3. **Bluesky** — where a lot of the science / bioinformatics / R community moved. Very active #rstats and genomics crowds.
4. (Optional) ORCID if you'll publish academically; Mastodon (fosstodon.org) if you lean open-source.

Use the **same name, same one-liner, same photo** across all of them. Repetition is the brand.

---

## 3. Content engine: one idea, three places

You already have the hard part — the blog. The trick is to not let a post be a one-shot. Each post becomes 3 things:

1. **The blog post** (the home base you own — no algorithm can take it away)
2. **A short LinkedIn/Bluesky post** — 3–4 sentences: the one insight + a link
3. **A reusable snippet** — push the code to a GitHub repo or gist

Write once, show up three times. This is how small efforts compound.

**Content buckets to rotate** (so you never stare at a blank page):
- **"TIL" (Today I Learned)** — one concept that clicked. 150–300 words. Your bread and butter.
- **Walkthroughs** — a real analysis, start to finish.
- **Tool/workflow** — your R setup, a package you love, a snippet.
- **Reflections** — what a month/project taught you. These travel furthest on LinkedIn.

---

## 4. A gentle 30-day starter plan

Designed to be light. If a week is hard, **skip it — the plan waits for you.** Missing a week is not failure; quitting is, and you can always restart.

- **Week 1 — Foundation.** Launch the site (Level 1 in `GUIDE.md`). Set up LinkedIn + Bluesky with the same handle/photo/one-liner. Don't post yet.
- **Week 2 — First signal.** Publish one short "TIL" post. Share it once on Bluesky and once on LinkedIn. That's the whole week.
- **Week 3 — Engage, don't broadcast.** Reply to 3 posts from people in #rstats / bioinformatics. Audiences are built by being a good community member, not by shouting. Optionally publish one more short post.
- **Week 4 — Repeat + reflect.** One post. Then write 3 lines somewhere about what you noticed. Decide your sustainable rhythm (e.g. "one short post every other week"). Lock that in.

Realistic target: **one small thing per week**. That's ~50 posts a year. Almost no one keeps that up — which is exactly why the few who do stand out.

## 5. Things that quietly build credibility

- **Pin your best post** on your profiles.
- **Keep GitHub tidy** — a couple of repos with a clear README beat 30 abandoned ones. Your blog's code snippets can each become a small repo.
- **A clean profile photo + banner** used everywhere. (I can help make a simple banner if you want.)
- **Add the blog link** to your GitHub profile, LinkedIn, and email signature.

---

## 6. Mindset guardrails (important)

- **Audience size is a vanity metric early on.** Ten of the right readers (peers, future collaborators, hiring managers) beat a thousand random follows. Don't measure yourself by the count.
- **Comparison is the fastest way to quit.** Everyone polished was once where you are; you're just doing it where people can see, which is braver, not lesser.
- **Define "enough" in advance.** "I posted one small thing this week" = a win. Full stop. Protect that definition on the low-energy days.
- **The blog is yours.** Social platforms come and go; the site you own is the asset. Everything else just points back to it.
